The Geometry of Chunks

Before a RAG system can answer anything, it has to cut your documents into pieces. How you cut them decides what it can find. Here are eleven ways to do it, in plain terms, with a guide to which one fits your industry.

§1Why chunking matters more than you think

Picture turning a textbook into a stack of index cards so you can find things fast. Make the cards too big and each one covers so much that you can't tell what it's about. Make them too small and an explanation gets torn across three cards. Cut in the wrong places and a sentence starts on one card and ends on the next.

That is chunking. A RAG system splits documents into chunks, turns each chunk into a vector (a list of numbers that captures its meaning), and at question time grabs the handful of chunks whose vectors sit closest to the question's. The language model only ever sees those chunks.

So a good chunk has to do three things at once:

  • Hold the whole answer. If the answer is split across two chunks, the model only gets half of it. Bigger chunks help here.
  • Be easy to find. A chunk about one thing matches questions about that thing. A chunk about five things matches nothing well. Smaller chunks help here.
  • Fit the budget. The model can only read so much. Bigger chunks mean fewer of them fit, and more of what fits is noise.

Those pull in opposite directions, and every strategy below is a different way of balancing them.

§2Three rules of thumb (with a little math)

Rule 1: Big chunks water down the signal

An embedding is roughly an average of everything in the chunk. If one sentence answers the question and the other fifteen are about something else, that sentence's signal gets averaged away. In a simple model, a relevant sentence scores about

\[ \text{match score} \;\approx\; \frac{\text{score of the relevant sentence}}{\sqrt{\text{number of sentences}}} \]

So one relevant sentence inside a 16-sentence chunk scores about a quarter of what it would score on its own. That is why "just make the chunks bigger" stops working.

Rule 2: Overlap stops answers getting cut in half

With chunks of size \(C\) and no overlap, an answer \(\ell\) tokens long gets split whenever it happens to cross a boundary. The chance it survives in one piece is

\[ P(\text{answer fits in one chunk}) = 1 - \frac{\ell}{C} \]

A 100-token answer in 512-token chunks gets cut about 20% of the time. Add an overlap \(O\) (each chunk repeats the last \(O\) tokens of the one before), and once the overlap is at least as long as the answer, nothing gets cut.

Rule of thumb

Make the overlap about as long as a typical answer, not a fixed percentage. Short FAQ answers need very little overlap. Long legal clauses need a lot, and at that point it's cheaper to split along the clauses themselves (see "Follow the document").

The price of overlap is storage: a 64-token overlap on 512-token chunks means about 14% more chunks to embed and store.

Rule 3: Aim for about three times the answer length

Put Rules 1 and 2 together in a toy model (bigger chunks keep more answers whole but get harder to find), and the sweet spot lands at

\[ \text{best chunk size} \;\approx\; 3 \times \text{typical answer length} \]

It's a simplification, but the lesson holds up: pick chunk size from how long your answers are, not from how much your embedding model can accept. Short factual answers (~60 tokens) point to ~180-token chunks. Long clauses (~250 tokens) point to ~750.

§4Eleven ways to chunk, from simplest to smartest

Each one comes with a quick summary: what it does, how much work it takes to set up, what it costs to run, and where it shines.

4.1 Fixed-size windows

In one line
Cut every N tokens
Setup effort
Very low
Running cost
Lowest
Shines on
Logs, raw transcripts

Chop the text every 512 tokens (say), with a bit of overlap. It's fast, predictable, and completely blind to meaning, so it cuts sentences, tables, and even words in half. Headings get separated from the text they introduce, leaving chunks like "must be filed within 30 days" with no clue what must be filed.

Still, it's the right tool when text has no real structure, like server logs or unpunctuated speech-to-text. And it's the baseline everything else should beat.

Good for: IT operations and telecom logs · call-centre transcripts · a quick first test in any industry.

4.2 Recursive splitting

In one line
Paragraphs first, then smaller
Setup effort
Low
Running cost
Low
Shines on
Help articles, wikis

Try to split on paragraph breaks. If a piece is still too big, split it on line breaks, then sentences, then words, and only chop mid-word as a last resort. It's like a careful editor who would always rather break at a paragraph than mid-sentence.

This is the best cheap default. For most everyday documents it beats fixed windows with almost no extra effort.

Good for: SaaS help centres · internal wikis · support tickets and email threads · the first version of almost any RAG system.

4.3 Sentence packing

In one line
Whole sentences, evenly sized
Setup effort
Low
Running cost
Low
Shines on
News, reviews

Never cut a sentence, and pack sentences into chunks of roughly equal size. Even sizes matter more than you'd expect: by Rule 1, short chunks score higher just for being short, so a chunker that leaves a few tiny scraps ends up with those scraps crowding the results. It's the same trick word processors use to avoid one very short line at the end of a paragraph.

Good for: news and media archives · product reviews in e-commerce · punctuated call transcripts in sales.

4.4 Follow the document's structure

In one line
Split by headings and sections
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
Low
Shines on
Contracts, filings, manuals

Most business documents already come divided up: chapters, sections, clauses, headings. Use those divisions. Keep each section together if it fits. If it doesn't, split it into its subsections, and keep going until everything fits. You end up cutting as few times as possible, and always along lines the author chose.

The bonus trick is a breadcrumb: start each chunk with where it lives, like Services Agreement › Section 9 Liability › 9.2 Cap. It costs a few tokens, makes chunks easier to find, and lets the model cite exactly where an answer came from.

The catch: it's only as good as the document parsing. A PDF with two columns and footnotes needs cleaning up before any of this works.

Good for: legal contracts and case law · financial filings (10-Ks, annual reports) · clinical guidelines · aerospace and manufacturing manuals · API documentation.

4.5 Split where the topic changes

In one line
Cut when meaning shifts
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
Medium
Shines on
Podcasts, lectures

Turn each sentence into a vector and compare every sentence with the next. When two neighbours suddenly look very different, the topic has probably changed, so cut there. It's like noticing the moment a conversation moves from sport to work.

It works well on text that drifts between topics but has no headings. Two things to watch: it doesn't limit chunk size on its own, so always set a maximum. And on text where everything is on the same topic (a pile of contracts, say), it finds "changes" that aren't really there.

Good for: podcasts, lectures and webinars in media and education · long essays · messy scraped web pages.

4.6 Whole-document segmentation

In one line
Find the best cuts overall
Setup effort
Higher
Running cost
Medium
Shines on
Meetings, patient histories

The previous method decides each cut on its own. This one looks at the whole document and picks the set of cuts that gives the most on-topic chunks overall. A single tuning knob sets how willing it is to add another cut. It's the difference between cutting a video wherever the shot changes and editing the whole thing so each scene holds together.

It's steadier than the local version, especially on long, unstructured conversations where the same topic comes back more than once.

Good for: earnings calls and board meetings in finance · long patient histories in healthcare · chat exports in legal investigations.

4.7 Late chunking

In one line
Read everything, then split
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
Medium
Shines on
Research papers, patents

Normal chunking embeds each chunk in isolation, so a chunk saying "Its revenue grew 12%" has no idea what "its" means. Late chunking flips the order. A long-context embedding model reads the whole document first, and only then are the results divided into chunks. Every chunk's vector already "knows" what came before and after it.

You get small, precise chunks that still carry the document's context, without paying an LLM to rewrite anything.

Good for: scientific papers in pharma R&D · patents · encyclopaedia-style pages · multilingual government documents.

4.8 Contextual chunks

In one line
Add a "you are here" note
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
LLM call per chunk
Shines on
Finance, insurance

Same problem, different fix: have an LLM write a sentence or two in front of each chunk to explain where it came from. "The Company's gross margin fell 2 points" becomes "From ACME's Q2 2025 quarterly report, results section: The Company's gross margin fell 2 points."

Anthropic's "Contextual Retrieval" write-up reported this cut failed retrievals by about a third, and by about two-thirds combined with keyword search and reranking. It costs one LLM call per chunk when you build the index, but prompt caching makes that much cheaper.

Good for: financial reports full of "the Company" and "the quarter" · insurance policies where the same clause means different things in different policies · big company knowledge bases with many near-duplicate documents.

4.9 Small-to-big (parent–child) and RAPTOR

In one line
Search small, read big
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
Low–medium
Shines on
Long reports, books

This one sidesteps the whole trade-off. Search over small chunks (easy to find, per Rule 1), but when one matches, hand the model its bigger parent section (enough context, per Rule 2). It's like using a book's index to find the page, then reading the whole page.

RAPTOR goes further. It groups similar chunks, has an LLM summarise each group, then groups and summarises the summaries, building a tree. Big-picture questions ("what are the main risks?") match a summary, and detailed ones match an original chunk.

Good for: drug approval dossiers in pharma · government policy and legislation · textbooks and courses · due-diligence data rooms in M&A.

4.10 One fact per chunk (propositions)

In one line
Rewrite into single facts
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
Highest
Shines on
Drug info, product specs

Rule 1 taken to the extreme: if mixing things together blurs the signal, make every chunk exactly one fact. An LLM rewrites the text into short standalone statements, spelling out what "it" and "they" refer to. "Its revenue grew 12%" becomes "ACME's 2025 revenue grew 12%."

You get very precise matches, but a lot more chunks. A knowledge base that makes 22,000 normal chunks can turn into 400,000 facts. It's worth it where one wrong neighbouring sentence could do real harm.

Good for: drug labels and dosing in healthcare · product specs and compatibility in e-commerce · regulatory obligations in banking and energy.

4.11 Special cases: code and tables

In one line
Respect the format
Setup effort
Medium
Running cost
Low
Shines on
Codebases, spreadsheets

Code: split by function and class, never mid-function. Start each chunk with its file name and function signature, since that's what developers search for.

Tables: a number means nothing without its column header. When a table is split into groups of rows, repeat the header in every chunk. For very wide tables, turn each row into a sentence ("Product: X; Price: $12.50; Region: EU"). Also add one summary chunk describing the table as a whole.

Good for: coding assistants · financial statements and rate tables · parts lists in manufacturing.

§5How to tell if it works

Don't pick a chunker on vibes. The good news is that testing one is simple:

  1. Collect 200 or so real questions and mark where in your documents each answer actually is.
  2. Keep everything else fixed: same embedding model, same number of results, same amount of text sent to the model.
  3. For each chunker, check two things: how much of the answer text made it into the results (recall), and how much of the results was actually the answer rather than filler (precision).
  4. Pick the one with the best recall, then use cost to break ties.

Measure by answer text, not by "was the right chunk in the top 5?" Chunks mean different things at different sizes, so counting chunks makes strategies impossible to compare fairly.

Keep an eye on cost

Strategies differ enormously in price. On a 10M-token knowledge base, plain 512-token chunks give about 22,000 vectors (around 90 MB). One-fact-per-chunk gives about 400,000 (around 1.6 GB). Contextual chunks and propositions are the only strategies that call an LLM while building the index, so use them where testing shows they pay off.

Or the five-line version:

  1. Check how long your answers are. Make chunks about 3× that, with overlap about 1×.
  2. If the document has structure, use it. It's cheap and gives you citations.
  3. Search small, read big. Parent–child gets you both with little effort.
  4. Only pay for LLM help where testing shows chunks are confusing on their own.
  5. Test on real questions before and after every change.
